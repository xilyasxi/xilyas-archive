ESSAY // TECHNOLOGY / HUMAN AGENCY

What Parts of Ourselves Are We Gradually Handing Over to Machines?

An essay about memory, attention, judgment, creativity, and the abilities we may gradually stop practicing as machines become better at performing them for us.

FORM ESSAY
SUBJECT TECHNOLOGY / HUMAN AGENCY
LOCATION JAPAN

Most mornings, before the day has properly started, we turn to a machine. We check our plans, see where we need to be, read new messages, and find out what happened while we were asleep. Our phones remember appointments, save conversations, suggest routes, recommend music, correct our writing, and show us information before we have decided to look for it.

Technology often makes daily life easier. A machine remembers because we might forget. It calculates because calculation can take time. It recommends because there are too many choices to consider. It can produce a first draft when we do not know how to begin. These uses can be valuable. Technology can make the world more accessible, reduce tiring work, and give people opportunities they might not otherwise have.

However, when a machine performs the same task for us again and again, it may do more than remove an inconvenience. It may begin to replace an ability that once developed through ordinary human practice.

This change is not always easy to notice. Using a tool does not automatically mean losing a skill. A calculator does not stop us from understanding numbers. Following directions does not mean that we have lost all sense of direction. A spellchecker can correct a mistake without taking away our ability to write.

Still, a skill may be more than something we can use when necessary. It may also depend on regular practice. Memory, attention, judgment, and imagination develop through use. If a system performs these activities whenever they become difficult, we may still be able to perform them in theory while becoming less used to performing them in everyday life.

Memory shows this clearly.

Digital tools allow us to preserve more of our lives than people could have imagined in the past. We take photographs, save messages, record conversations, store documents, and receive reminders about birthdays and appointments. This kind of external memory can protect us from loss. It can preserve family histories and personal experiences that might otherwise disappear.

But remembering is not the same as retrieving a record.

Human memory does not simply store information. It chooses what to keep, connects events, and changes over time. It gives experiences a place within the rest of our lives. A photograph can show us where we were, but it cannot decide what that moment meant to us. An archive can tell us what happened, but it cannot recreate the experience of carrying that memory through time.

There is a difference between knowing that something is available and carrying it within oneself. Someone may have thousands of photographs and still have no clear sense of a particular period until an image brings part of it back. The photograph has preserved the event. It may also have allowed the person to delay the slower process through which the event becomes part of their understanding of their own life.

What happens when we preserve everything but allow less of it to become memory?

A machine can keep the details of our lives. It cannot decide which details should shape us.

A similar issue appears in the way we use our attention. Machines increasingly help determine what we notice. Notifications tell us what may need a response. Feeds organize information according to what we are predicted to like. Recommendation systems choose which songs, articles, programmes, products, and opinions should appear next.

This is not always harmful. No one has enough time to search through everything. A useful recommendation can save time and introduce us to something we would not have found on our own.

But selection is never completely neutral. When a system decides what is most likely to hold our attention, it also affects what we do not see. We may feel that we are choosing from the world when we are actually choosing from a smaller, personalized version of it. Unfamiliar things become less likely to appear because the system has not yet learned that we may be interested in them.

Does curiosity remain fully independent when it is constantly predicted?

A recommendation can certainly lead to a new interest. The problem is not that every recommendation is harmful. The problem is that there is a difference between being guided toward something and being shown only what has already been judged suitable for us. One kind of help introduces an opportunity. The other may prevent us from encountering opportunities that the system did not expect.

This does not mean that unfiltered attention is always better. Human attention is limited, and tools that remove unnecessary information can help us concentrate. The important question is whether we are using these tools to pursue what we have decided matters, or allowing them to decide what matters before we have made that judgment ourselves.

The issue becomes more serious when machines move from organizing information to evaluating choices. A navigation system does not merely show us possible roads; it suggests a route. A streaming service does not simply offer a catalogue; it predicts what we will enjoy. An automated system may assess a job applicant, identify a medical risk, or recommend a financial decision. An artificial intelligence system can provide an answer before we have fully developed our own.

There is a difference between giving a machine a calculation and giving it a judgment.

A machine may be better than a person at processing large amounts of information. It may notice patterns that an individual could not find. But judgment involves more than identifying the most likely result. It also involves context, values, responsibility, and the ability to ask whether the available choices are appropriate in the first place.

A recommendation can influence a decision without officially becoming the decision. We remain free to reject it. Yet that freedom may be limited when we do not have the time, confidence, or knowledge needed to question the recommendation.

The answer arrives already organized. It can seem objective partly because we did not see how it was produced.

A person may accept a system’s judgment and still feel that the decision was entirely their own. Where, then, does responsibility belong? Does it belong to the person who accepted the result, the people who designed the system, or the data from which the system learned? Responsibility becomes harder to identify when a machine makes a choice appear impersonal.

Creative work raises a similar problem in a more personal area of life. Machines can now help people write, compose, design, translate, and create images. This can be useful and freeing. Someone without technical training may be able to express an idea that would otherwise be difficult to communicate. Someone who cannot find a starting point may use a generated draft to begin.

However, creating something can matter for reasons beyond the final product. Writing, revising, and struggling often help us discover what we think. An idea may not be fully formed at the beginning. It may become clearer through the process of working on it. A difficult sentence can show that an argument is incomplete. An awkward image can reveal something about what we are trying to express.

If a machine produces clear and convincing language before we have worked through these difficulties, it may solve the immediate problem while taking away part of the process through which we might have understood our own ideas.

Does this make machine-assisted work dishonest or inauthentic? Not necessarily. Human creativity has always involved tools, influences, cooperation, and existing forms. Very few people create entirely alone. The more important question is whether the person remains involved in developing and shaping the meaning of the work, or whether they simply accept the first result that sounds reasonable.

A machine can help someone express an idea. It cannot decide whether the idea is worth expressing, what the person is responsible for saying, or why the expression matters.

Clear language is not the same as understanding.

A carefully written apology produced by a system may communicate regret. It may also allow someone to avoid facing what they have done. The words can be appropriate while the person who uses them has not really considered their meaning.

The idea that machines are “taking parts of ourselves” should still be treated carefully. Delegating a task is not always a loss. Technology can increase a person’s independence. Assistive devices can make movement and communication possible. Translation tools can help people communicate across languages. Automated systems can reduce exhausting labour. Educational tools can provide access to knowledge that was once difficult to reach.

A person does not become less human by using a tool. Self-reliance is not always the most important goal. Some tasks should be delegated. Removing repetitive work can give people more time for care, thought, and creativity. There is no reason to preserve every difficulty simply because it is difficult.

The important question is what happens to a person’s ability to direct the activity.

Does the machine support someone who understands and controls the task, or does it make the task unnecessary? Does it expand the possible choices, or reduce them to whatever the system can predict? Can the person question the result? Can they correct it? Can they explain why they accepted it? Can they take responsibility for what happens next?

Different abilities may need to be treated in different ways. Delegating arithmetic may be harmless if we still understand the problem. A navigation system may be useful if we can recognize when it has made an error. Translation can make basic communication possible, but it may be less reliable when cultural knowledge or moral sensitivity is important. A medical tool can assist a decision without replacing the human relationship in which that decision is explained and discussed.

The boundary will not be the same for everyone. A person may reasonably depend on a tool because of disability, circumstance, or limited access. A serious account of human ability should not confuse unnecessary struggle with virtue. The issue is not whether everyone should perform every task without help. It is whether the arrangement supports a person’s ability to understand, question, and act.

The central difference may be between having a skill available and actually practicing it.

A person may still be able to remember, judge, write, or navigate in principle. But abilities that are rarely used can become weaker. They remain available for emergencies rather than forming part of everyday life. We know that we could perform a task if necessary, but the task no longer feels like a normal part of what we do.

This is why the question is not only what machines are taking from us. It is also what we are choosing not to practice.

We are deciding, often without discussing it directly, which activities will remain part of ordinary human life and which will be treated as unnecessary effort. Schools, workplaces, families, and individuals all face this decision. They must consider when assistance helps develop competence and when it makes competence unnecessary.

Some difficulties are simply obstacles. Others are part of understanding. The effort involved in remembering can create attachment. The effort involved in paying attention can make us notice more. The effort involved in judging can create a sense of responsibility. The effort involved in making something can help us understand what we think.

It is easy to ask what machines will be able to do next. It may be more difficult to ask what we will eventually stop knowing how to do because machines have become so good at doing it for us.

Machines will continue to store, calculate, predict, generate, and recommend. They will perform these tasks with increasing speed. But they cannot decide what we should care about, what responsibility requires, or which experiences should be allowed to change us.

People can give up those forms of judgment. They can allow systems to determine what is remembered, noticed, valued, and expressed. The danger is not necessarily that machines will become more human. It is that people may become less practiced in the activities through which they understand themselves.

We are not simply handing pieces of ourselves to machines. We are redistributing effort, attention, and responsibility. Sometimes this gives us more freedom. At other times, it makes us dependent on systems we do not fully understand.

The important question is whether the arrangement leaves us able to understand, question, revise, and take responsibility for what machines help us do.

The future of human agency may not require us to refuse technological assistance. It may require us to recognize which abilities need to remain part of our lives as regular practices, rather than existing only as functions that are available when needed.

A machine can remember for us, but it cannot care about what is remembered. It can recommend, but it cannot decide what is worth choosing. It can produce language, but it cannot take responsibility for what we mean.

What we hand over may depend less on what machines can do than on what we gradually stop doing ourselves.
