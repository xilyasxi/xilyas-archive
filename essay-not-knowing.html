ESSAY // PHILOSOPHICAL REFLECTION

The Discipline of Not Knowing

On uncertainty, judgment, restraint, and the responsibility of refusing to claim an answer that has not been earned.

FORM ESSAY
SUBJECT UNCERTAINTY / JUDGMENT
STATUS COMPLETE

Uncertainty is often treated as a condition that should eventually be overcome.

A question is asked. Information is gathered. A conclusion is reached.

The conclusion may not be perfect, but it is usually considered better than continued doubt. To remain uncertain can look like weakness, confusion, or a failure to understand.

But certainty is not always valuable because it is true.

Sometimes it is valuable because it makes action easier. It gives a person the feeling that reality has become orderly enough to manage. An answer closes the question, even when it does not fully explain what the question was asking.

This creates a conflict between two legitimate responsibilities.

One is the responsibility to judge. A person cannot remain suspended between possibilities forever. Decisions must sometimes be made with incomplete information, and refusing to choose may have consequences of its own.

The other is the responsibility to remain honest about the limits of one’s knowledge. A person should not turn an assumption into a fact simply because action would be more comfortable if the distinction disappeared.

Neither responsibility is simply an error.

Judgment protects the ability to act. Restraint protects the ability to think.

The difficulty begins when one is treated as though it could exist without the other.

A person who insists on certainty may appear strong because he speaks clearly and without hesitation. But clarity of expression is not the same as accuracy of thought.

Confidence can create the appearance of knowledge without possessing its substance.

A conclusion may sound convincing because it is delivered quickly, firmly, or with authority. None of these qualities proves that the conclusion is correct.

The desire for a definite answer can also shorten the process of understanding. Some questions are not difficult because no answer exists. They are difficult because the answer cannot be separated from conditions, interpretations, or limits.

To close such a question too quickly may provide relief without providing knowledge.

The person who demands an immediate conclusion may not be seeking truth so much as an end to the discomfort of not knowing.

Still, uncertainty cannot become an excuse for permanent hesitation.

Doubt may be used to avoid judgment, commitment, or responsibility. A person can continue examining possibilities long after the available evidence is sufficient for a reasonable decision. In that case, uncertainty is no longer a discipline of thought.

It becomes a way of remaining untouched by the consequences of choosing.

This is why not knowing must be distinguished from refusing to know.

Not knowing can involve careful attention to what has and has not been established. Refusing to know may involve ignoring evidence because certainty is inconvenient. The first requires humility. The second may require avoidance.

The fact that complete knowledge is impossible does not mean that all beliefs are equally justified.

Judgment becomes most important when certainty is unavailable.

If action required complete knowledge, few meaningful decisions could ever be made. A person must often act according to evidence, reason, and responsibility without receiving a guarantee about the outcome.

This does not make knowledge irrelevant.

It makes the use of knowledge more difficult.

A judgment made under uncertainty should contain more than a conclusion. It should contain an awareness of its own basis and its own limits.

What is known?

What is inferred?

What is assumed?

What evidence would change the decision? What consequences would follow if the judgment were wrong?

These questions do not eliminate uncertainty, but they prevent uncertainty from being hidden beneath the appearance of confidence.

The same conflict appears beyond the individual.

A group needs enough agreement to act, but too much agreement may prevent disagreement from being heard. An institution needs procedures to make judgment consistent, but procedures can conceal uncertainty behind official language. A society needs shared conclusions in order to coordinate, but a society that treats every established belief as unquestionable may lose the ability to correct itself.

At each level, the problem is similar.

Action requires a degree of confidence, but confidence can become dangerous when it forgets that it is provisional. Doubt makes correction possible, but doubt can also prevent any decision from being made.

The values cannot always be preserved equally.

Sometimes delay carries the greater risk. Sometimes action would cause harm that cannot be repaired. The responsible choice depends on the circumstances, the quality of the evidence, and the consequences of being wrong.

The responsible person is therefore not the one who always chooses certainty. Nor is he the one who always protects uncertainty.

He is the one who can recognize what the circumstances require.

He knows when evidence is strong enough to support action and when caution is necessary because the cost of error is too great. He can make a judgment without pretending that the judgment is infallible.

Perhaps the discipline of not knowing is not the discipline of remaining without an answer.

It is the discipline of refusing to claim an answer that has not been earned.

Its purpose is not to prevent action, but to make action more honest.

The strongest mind may not be the one that speaks with complete assurance. It may be the one that can remain steady between the need to decide and the knowledge that certainty has not yet arrived.
